Here is the problem. A boat moves at three metres per second relative to still water. A uniform river current flows along the bank, from right to left. Which heading makes the boat's path from one bank to the other as short as possible? Consider currents of two and four metres per second.

Start with the slower current. Pause the video and predict the best heading. Pointing downstream adds drift, while pointing directly upstream gives no motion across. Pointing straight across maximizes the cross-river speed, so it gives the fastest crossing. But the current makes the actual path diagonal. Fastest is not shortest. The shortest path between parallel banks is perpendicular, so the boat's actual velocity, not merely its heading, must point straight across.

Let vector u be the current velocity and vector v the boat's velocity relative to the water. The actual velocity is their sum: vector w equals vector u plus vector v. Draw the current vector with length two, pointing downstream. From its tip, draw every possible boat-velocity vector. They all have length three, so their endpoints form a circle of radius three. Every possible resultant starts at the origin and ends on this circle. For the shortest path, choose a resultant that points straight across. The perpendicular line intersects the circle, so this is possible.

The upstream component of the boat's velocity must cancel the current. If alpha is the angle between the boat's heading and the bank, then three cosine alpha equals two. Therefore, cosine alpha equals two thirds, giving alpha approximately forty-eight point two degrees. Point the boat forty-eight point two degrees to the bank, aimed upstream. Its actual speed is approximately two point two four metres per second, and its path is straight across: the absolute shortest route.

Now increase the current to four metres per second. The circle's centre moves to four, while its radius stays three. The perpendicular line through the origin misses the circle. The current is too strong to cancel, so a straight-across path is impossible. Choose the reachable direction that comes closest to perpendicular. In the velocity diagram, this is the line from the origin tangent to the circle. At the tangent point, the boat-velocity vector is perpendicular to the resultant.

The tangent construction gives a right triangle. If beta is the angle between the boat's heading and the bank on the upstream side, then cosine beta equals three fourths, so beta is approximately forty-one point four degrees. The boat still moves downstream as it crosses. Its resultant speed is approximately two point six five metres per second, and its path length is four thirds of the river width.

In both cases, point the boat upstream. With a current of two metres per second, aim forty-eight point two degrees to the bank and cancel the drift. With a current of four metres per second, aim forty-one point four degrees and accept some downstream drift. The key is to distinguish the heading from the actual path.
